## 5. Responsible use

Three rules governed the use of AI tools, and they are the reason the log can be
specific.

1. **Nothing was accepted on the assistant's word.** AI-written code was run against the
   project's data, and its output compared with a figure computed by another route. The
   dashboard is the clearest case: the assistant built it, and it was then checked by
   querying the loaded model and subtracting the R pipeline's own numbers from it.
2. **The assistant was corrected when it was wrong.** It kept a map from the first
   iteration that was bound to the wrong country's geography (E4-02), and its first draft
   of report sections 5 and 7 overran the page budget. Both are recorded above rather
   than quietly fixed.
3. **Personal and evaluative writing stays personal.** The reflection was redrafted by
   its author (AI-M4-C6), and the ethics decisions in section 2 record judgements the
   team made, written down with assistance rather than decided by it.

## 6. Reflection - Timothy Wubbeling (Person C)

Milestone 4 was the first time the whole project had to agree with itself. My job was not new analysis but compiling it: merging two dashboards onto the final dataset, writing sections 5 and 7, and making the report, the app and the dashboard quote one set of numbers. Two correct referral rates, 58.9% on the test set and 59.2% across all households, could have looked like a mistake; I labelled both.

What surprised me was how much of that was judgement rather than technique. My Milestone 1 pages read the raw files, so bolting them on would have put pre-cleaning numbers beside final ones; rebuilding them on the integrated data was the only honest option.

What changed is where I think accuracy comes from. I trust a figure only when two independent paths produce it. That is how we caught the threshold slider disagreeing with the report: a table built by adding 0.01 at a time stored 0.24 as slightly more than 0.24, dropping 26 households.

What I would improve: I left reconciliation until the dashboard existed, and the page budget until the report was written. Both are cheap to write first and expensive to apply last.

## 7. Reflection - Alexander Solomon (Person A)

What worked best for this project is that we split it into 3, so each person did a specific section of each milestone. Because I built the modelling and then wrote about it, I could explain every choice. Writing down in Milestone 2 that we would fall back to a binary target if the middle tier couldn't be separated also paid off. When the three-tier models failed to find that tier, we switched without scrambling.

The biggest surprise was how much went wrong in such small, practical places. My leakage checks failed because I assumed the integrated file was already trimmed, when it still had 128 columns. Later, I ran renv::init() in the wrong project folder and had to redo it. Across the milestones, the target and the final model changed more than I expected, and the project grew from an analysis into a tool with monitoring.

I learned how tidymodels recipes keep preprocessing inside each cross-validation fold, and how to work with Git and GitHub as a team. Next time, I would agree on the model's output format with whoever builds the app much earlier, because the three-tier vs binary mismatch cost us time, and I would set up renv on the first day.

## 8. Reflection - Rudi Jan du Plessis (Person B)

What worked was being handed a model I had not fitted and having to judge it on its own terms. Writing the threshold rule down before I looked at the test set - at least 80% of at-risk households, at the fewest referrals - meant 0.24 was a decision I could defend rather than one I found afterwards, and it survived every challenge in review.

The surprise was how often two correct numbers can contradict each other. 58.9% of test households referred and 59.2% across all of them are both right, and I nearly let them sit in one paragraph as if they were the same figure. That is why I wrote check_numbers.py, which recomputes the headline metrics and fails if an easily confused pair gets swapped. The second surprise was the grant result reversing: households receiving grants are more food insecure, not less, and the honest thing was to publish it as targeting rather than quietly drop it.

What changed is that I stopped trusting a number because a notebook produced it. I turned down the random forest and its extra 0.009 of AUC, because the tool has to explain every referral to an official. Next time I would build the reconciliation check in the first week, not the last.
